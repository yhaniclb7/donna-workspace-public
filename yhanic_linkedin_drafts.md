## DRAFT POST #1
**Pillar:** From Cockpit to Boardroom  
**Theme:** Ejection as a metaphor for calculated risk

---

I ejected from an F/A-18 at 300 knots.

May 2023. Clear day over the Pacific. One moment I'm in control of a $70M fighter jet, the next I'm hanging under a parachute watching it spiral into the ocean.

Here's what nobody tells you about ejecting: you don't do it because you failed. You do it because staying in the cockpit is worse.

**The business parallel?**

Too many founders I meet are riding their own aircraft into the ground because they're afraid to "eject" from:
• The bad hire they keep hoping will improve
• The product feature that isn't working
• The pivot they know they need to make

Ejection isn't failure. It's survival. It's the discipline to recognize when your current trajectory is unrecoverable and act decisively.

In my 11 years as a Naval Aviator, I learned that the pilots who survive aren't the ones who never have emergencies—they're the ones who train for them, recognize them early, and execute their emergency procedures without ego.

Since transitioning to VC and building my own ventures, I see the same pattern. The founders who win are the ones who eject from bad deals, wrong markets, and sunk costs faster than their competition.

Your ego will tell you to stay with the aircraft. Your training should tell you when to leave.

What's your "ejection handle" in business? The thing you know you should pull but haven't yet?

## DRAFT POST #2
**Pillar:** Deal Flow Diary  
**Theme:** Pattern recognition in VC — what military training teaches you about spotting winners

---

I reviewed 47 pitch decks this month.

Three got meetings. One got a term sheet.

The pattern? It wasn't the TAM slides or the hockey-stick projections. It was something I first learned to recognize in the cockpit: **composure under uncertainty.**

When you're inbound to a carrier at night, in weather, with low fuel, you don't have complete information. You can't. You have instruments, briefings, and your training—but ultimately, you're making a decision with incomplete data under pressure.

Sound familiar, founders?

Here's what I look for in founders that I first learned to recognize in fellow pilots:

**1. Brief calmness**
The best pilots aren't emotionless—they're focused. When everything's going wrong, they get quieter, not louder. Same with founders. The ones who raise their voice when a server crashes or a deal falls through? Pass.

**2. Systems thinking**
In aviation, we call it "cockpit resource management." In startups, it's knowing which levers actually matter. Great founders don't just solve problems—they build systems that prevent recurrence.

**3. Brief honesty**
Pilots who hide mistakes kill people. Founders who hide metrics kill companies. I always ask: "What are you not telling me?" The answer tells me everything.

The military trained me to make million-dollar decisions with seconds of analysis. VC isn't that different—just different stakes, same skills.

Fellow VCs: What pattern are you seeing in today's market that others are missing?

## DRAFT POST #3
**Pillar:** The Golden Prison + Building in Public  
**Theme:** Building multiple ventures while transitioning careers — the myth of "choosing one path"

---

"So which one are you actually doing?"

I get this question weekly. Usually from well-meaning people who genuinely can't understand why I'd simultaneously:
• Start at United Airlines (training in Denver now)
• Build an AI automation business
• Pursue VC/PE opportunities
• Finish my Kellogg EMBA

They think I'm unfocused. I think they're in golden prisons.

The golden prison is that comfortable career path that pays well, impresses people at parties, and slowly suffocates the part of you that wants to build something original. It's the airline captain who always dreamed of starting a business. The VC associate who wants to be a founder. The consultant who wants to create, not just advise.

I spent 11 years in the Navy. It was incredible. And I knew exactly when to eject.

Now I'm building a portfolio career—multiple bets, multiple learning curves, multiple ways to create value. Some will work. Some won't. That's the point.

My twin brother is an orthopedic surgeon. He's all-in on medicine. That works for him. I'm wired differently—I need multiple fires burning to feel alive.

The AI automation business? We're already processing workflows that save clients 15+ hours weekly. The airline career? 30-year runway with incredible benefits. The VC work? I'm learning deal structures that make me a better founder.

They're not competing priorities. They're compounding assets.

You don't have to choose one path. You have to choose YOUR path.

What's your golden prison? And what's the ejection handle?
